A response to [this article](https://www.thetimes.com/article/e6877320-0b98-456d-b0ae-575029f23dd7) in The Times: ***"Is A-Level music about to disappear from state schools?"***



There are obviously local questions of funding, staffing and institutional support, but these explanations only go so far. The more fundamental issue is that the musical culture which the subject was designed to serve has changed with extraordinary speed.



When I took A-level Music 17 years ago, the assumptions underpinning the subject were still relatively coherent. “Classical music” meant, broadly speaking, Beethoven, Brahms and the Western concert tradition; learning an instrument usually meant piano, violin, flute or something comparable; composition implied some degree of engagement with an inherited technical language. Those assumptions were already weakening, but they remained sufficiently intact for the subject to possess a reasonably stable idea of what musical achievement looked like.



That world has now fragmented. “Classical” or canonical repertoire in the classroom may encompass the Beatles or the Eagles; instrumental study increasingly means guitar, drum kit, popular voice or computer-based production; composition may mean anything from an academic pastiche of Mendelssohn to a rock song produced in a bedroom. None of those things is intrinsically illegitimate. The difficulty is that they arise from very different musical traditions, embody different ideas of skill and excellence, and are often being forced into a single system of assessment.



Music education therefore finds itself in a peculiar liminal state. One musical culture is frequently being judged according to criteria inherited from another, while the profession has not yet developed a convincing common language with which to compare them. How exactly does one compare a singer performing Adele with a singer performing Bach? Or a stylistically accomplished chorale with an electronically produced song? It can be done, but only by making fairly substantial assumptions about what musical knowledge, difficulty, originality and excellence actually mean.



And this exposes a deeper philosophical problem. Music, perhaps more nakedly than almost any other discipline, has traditionally depended upon hierarchy and value judgement. We speak instinctively of the greatest composers, the greatest works, the greatest singers, even the greatest guitar solos. Musical culture is saturated with distinctions between the ordinary and the exceptional. Yet contemporary cultural discourse is often deeply uncomfortable with precisely those kinds of hierarchical claims. In a culture increasingly committed to pluralism and relativism, it becomes much harder to articulate an authoritative vocabulary of musical value without appearing merely to impose the preferences of one tradition upon another.



A-level Music is therefore not simply suffering from bad policy. It is also a relic of an older cultural settlement in which particular prestige forms of music were institutionally preserved because their value was assumed rather than continuously justified. That settlement has largely disappeared, while the examination system built upon it remains.



This is why I think music in schools is unusually revealing. Western musical culture is becoming more plural, fragmented and diffuse, and school music sits directly on the fault line. The problems appearing in examinations, curricula and instrumental education are therefore not merely administrative problems. They are symptoms of a much larger civilisational transition in which we have greatly expanded the range of musical practices we are prepared to value, without arriving at any corresponding agreement about how different forms of musical excellence are to be understood or compared. 



The pupils are being asked to acquire skills from a tradition whose cultural authority has largely evaporated. To succeed at A-level Music, they still benefit enormously from what are essentially classical competencies: fluent notation, harmonic literacy, score reading, aural discrimination, instrumental technique, stylistic awareness and the ability to manipulate musical material in quite formal ways. Yet the surrounding culture no longer tells them that these things represent sophistication, prestige, seriousness or even particularly desirable forms of expertise. Instead, classical music is often encountered simply as something old, difficult and remote.



So there is a profound motivational incoherence built into the subject. The examination continues to reward the residue of an older musical hierarchy while the culture surrounding the pupil has ceased to provide the narrative that made that hierarchy intelligible. A teenager is effectively told: these skills matter enormously if you want to do well, but there is no obvious reason why they should matter more than the musical skills you already value.



So, in short, they're confused and they don't take the subject. I'm also confused. Everyone's confused.
