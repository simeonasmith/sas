Two years before the Skylon punctured the gloomy skies above London’s postwar South Bank, Theodor Adorno wrote that “to write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric.” His target was not vernacular culture alone, but the wider European cultural inheritance whose claims to refinement and civilisation had proved incapable of preventing barbarism. Yet the catastrophe placed particular strain on ideas of rootedness, nation, folk tradition and inherited form. The Skylon offered an especially potent repudiation of both: literally rootless, it seemed to float above the ground; formally, it dispensed with historical reference altogether in favour of pure abstraction and geometry.
